## Slide 1 — Proposed Solution

**What to include**

- State the problem clearly to show that the team understands it.
- Explain the proposed solution and how it addresses or resolves the problem.
- Describe what is innovative or unique about the idea.
- Use text, visuals, or both—choose the form that communicates each point best.

**Creator’s explanation and tips**

- Distinguish the solution itself (“this is the problem; this is our solution”) from how the solution resolves the problem.
- Keep all text purposeful. The example had substantial text, but each line added value.
- Make the solution feel compelling and clearly explain its value. The creator calls this “elevating” the product.
- Use an image when it communicates the problem or solution; an image is not required just for decoration.
- The examples from two finalist teams used different amounts of text and visuals. The creator’s test was whether the slide communicates the right thing, not how many diagrams or words it contains.
- Use AI as an assistant and stay in control of the content. The creator speculates that AI-based screening may be used and advises against AI-generated images; this is his caution, not a confirmed SIH rule.
- Do not buy someone else’s “winning PPT.” Learn what the slide needs to communicate. The creator says sharing examples for learning is useful, while selling winning decks is unethical.

## Slide 2 — Technical Approach and Architecture

**What to include**

- Name the technologies or components in the proposed stack.
- Explain why each technology was selected and what role it plays in the product.
- Show a flow diagram or architecture that explains how the system works, including a clear starting point and sequence.
- Where useful, add a prototype/demo video or GitHub link. The creator presents these as possible additions, not mandatory items.

**Creator’s explanation and tips**

- Avoid listing language or tool names as unexplained logos (for example, Python, Java, or C++). Connect each choice to its purpose and how it supports the product.
- Be ready to justify alternatives. If the deck says React, for instance, the evaluator may ask why the team chose it over Angular.
- Keep the flow diagram simple enough for a non-specialist to follow. The creator says even a non-technical evaluator should be able to understand the flow.
- Do not exaggerate the diagram beyond what the problem requires. Show the real process; an authentication flow, for example, should show the relevant login, request, server, and response steps.
- ChatGPT or Gemini can be used as references while building a prototype, but the creator advises not making AI the core of the solution.
- Tools he names for diagrams: Napkin AI, Excalidraw, draw.io, and Lucidchart.
- Record a demo video, according to the creator’s recommendation. He says it will be covered further in a later video.

## Slide 3 — Feasibility and Viability

**What to include**

- Explain whether the idea can actually be built (feasibility) and whether it can be sustained over time (viability).
- Address the relevant dimensions:
  - **Technical:** required capabilities, APIs, data, infrastructure, and resources.
  - **Financial:** expected costs and whether the team or deployment can afford them (such as API/token costs).
  - **Market:** whether there is a sustainable need or opportunity.
  - **Operational:** how the solution would be deployed, commercialized, and maintained.
- Identify potential challenges and risks, then pair each with a mitigation or response.

**Creator’s explanation and tips**

- Avoid unsupported claims such as calling everything “AI-powered” or describing capabilities the team cannot build.
- Be candid about weaknesses; no product is perfect.
- A challenge may not be solvable immediately, but the team should understand it and explain a credible plan to address it.
- Example from the video: fast-moving LEO satellites can cause Doppler changes; show the challenge and the corresponding mitigation.

## Slide 4 — Impact and Benefits

**What to include**

- Describe the real-world effect if the solution is deployed.
- Explain expected social and economic impact, benefits to target users or communities, and relevant strategic benefits.
- Where relevant, discuss costs or adoption barriers and how the team would address them.
- Use evidence, scenarios, or graphs when they help communicate the expected impact.

**Creator’s explanation and tips**

- Connect benefits to actual users and real-world scenarios rather than making generic claims.
- The examples use different formats, including visuals, text, and graphs. Choose the format that best explains the impact.
- The creator emphasizes communicating the correct information over using a particular number of diagrams or words.

## Slide 5 — Research and References

**What to include**

- Cite relevant, real research papers and other credible references related to the problem or solution.
- Identify existing solutions or prior work, even if those solutions address only part of the problem.
- Connect the research to the team’s understanding and design choices.

**Creator’s explanation and tips**

- Do not treat research and references as an afterthought, even when using AI.
- The creator names ScienceDirect and Elsevier as places to look for papers. Verify sources and cite the specific works used.
- The purpose is to demonstrate that the team has investigated existing work and understands the context.

## Requirements and presentation guidance across the deck

### Template and slide structure

- The creator’s strongest instruction is to start from the official SIH idea PPT template, available from the official Smart India Hackathon website under its guidelines.
- He says each slide corresponds to an evaluation criterion. Use the current official template and check its current rules directly; this video does not establish whether modifying the background, adding slides, or changing layouts is allowed.
- Keep the deck aligned with the official slide topics and evaluation criteria: innovation, proposed solution, feasibility, technical approach, architecture diagram, impact and benefits, and research/references.
- The examples show that teams can communicate the same topics with different visual/text treatments. Do not infer from this that official slide counts or template rules can be changed.

### Content quality

- Make every line, diagram, and image earn its place by helping explain the problem, solution, design, feasibility, or impact.
- Be accurate about what exists now versus what is a future plan. Avoid exaggerating capabilities.
- Explain the reasons behind technology choices and the risks behind the proposed plan.
- Present the information so that both technical and non-technical evaluators can follow it.

### Pitching the deck

- Prepare to explain the idea to both college staff and industry evaluators; the creator says the evaluator mix can vary.
- Know the audience where possible, and adapt the explanation to what they are likely to know.
- Use a clear pitch sequence: problem → solution → how the MVP works → what implementation would look like → business plan.
- Do not exaggerate during the pitch.
- The creator calls a business plan underrated because it shows thinking beyond the hackathon.
- Teams may divide the speaking parts among members, or choose one presenter; the creator says either can work.
